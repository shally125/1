# Why we are here

## my goal
I came to programming from a different direction. My original field is radio and television directing, with a focus on theatre. I am interested in making an XR theatre rehearsal system: a shared virtual space where actors, directors, designers, and other collaborators can rehearse and perform either remotely or physically together. At first, programming seemed like an additional technical skill that I needed in order to build this system. After beginning this course, I have started to see it differently. I am here not because programming is a more important form of creativity, or because I expect to become a professional software engineer. I am here because I need to understand the material of the space in which my future performances may happen.

## A Rehearsal Room Is Not a Prompt

My initial idea sounds simple: create an XR space in which actors, directors, and designers can rehearse online or enter the same virtual environment. However, this description is not yet a specification. It does not explain how an actor moves through the space, how a director changes blocking, or how a designer adjusts scenery during rehearsal. It says nothing about voice delay, body tracking, scale, or what happens when one participant does not have the same equipment as everyone else.

Learning basic programming can help me turn a vague artistic intention into questions about events, states, inputs, and consequences. What should happen when an actor touches a virtual object? Who can move that object? Should the change be visible immediately to every participant? Asking these questions is partly programming and partly directing. 

## Reading May Matter More Than Typing

I do not believe every designer must become a professional software engineer. For my practice, reading code may eventually be more important than producing every line from memory. However, reading is not passive. I need to know what information a system collects, which actions trigger responses, and where its assumptions are located. If AI generates a script that records an actor’s voice or physical movement, I should be able to notice this and ask whether that data is necessary. If a generated interaction fails during rehearsal, I need enough understanding to identify the problem instead of repeatedly asking the machine to “fix it.”

This resembles directing. A director does not perform every role or operate every piece of stage equipment, but cannot make responsible decisions without understanding what those roles and materials can do. Similarly, I may delegate much of the writing of code while remaining responsible for what the resulting system asks performers to do. Practicing small programs is how I can begin to develop the judgement needed to read larger machine-generated ones.

## Seeing a Stage as Data

In *The Art of Code*, Dylan Beattie presents programming as an expressive medium rather than only a practical tool . The example that interested me most was a depth-perception demonstration in which elements of a photograph were identified by depth, and reconstructed. It made me think about converting a stage-design image into a virtual space.

A designer could provide one image, and a system could estimate its depth and turn it into the beginning of an XR set. This could make visual experimentation faster and allow theatre-makers in different locations to enter a design before building it physically. It could also allow one stage image to be transformed into several possible spatial arrangements.

Understanding some programming would let me treat this technology as a material rather than a magic service. Like lighting, scenery, and editing, code changes what I notice and what I imagine to be possible. Without technical understanding, I might use XR only to imitate a physical rehearsal room. With it, I could explore spaces that change scale, respond to an actor’s voice, or rearrange themselves according to movement.

## What Survives

For my XR theatre practice, authorship does not depend on writing every line myself. It depends on whether I can explain why the system behaves as it does and take responsibility for how that behaviour affects actors, designers, directors, and audiences. I am here not because machines cannot write code. I am here because working code is not the same as the right work, and I need enough knowledge to recognise the difference.
